## Piling It High | New York City | 1930-1950
[film 8]
Stages of retail history unfold as footage rolls of old neighborhood corner stores with crowded wooden counters and clerks pulling items from high shelves.
Customers waited patiently while merchants measured out sugar and flour by hand from large burlap sacks.
Prices were rarely posted on goods, and shopping required a long, exhausting trip to multiple specialty shops.
[film 3.5]
Urban residents visited a local butcher for meat, a greengrocer for produce, and a bakery for daily bread.
This traditional European-style system of purchasing food had remained largely unchanged for generations.
[film 3.5]
It was slow, highly labor-intensive, and fundamentally expensive for ordinary working-class households.
The average urban family spent fully one-third of its entire budget just on food during the rigid nineteen-twenties.
[film 3.5]
Everything changed when early supermarkets opened inside former garages in spaces like Jamaica, Queens.
Operating under the logic of pile it high and sell it cheap, King Kullen introduced self-service and discount pricing.
[film 3.5]
Shoppers grabbed wire baskets and selected goods from open shelves where prices were clearly marked for all to see.
