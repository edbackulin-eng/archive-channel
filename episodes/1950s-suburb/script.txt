## Who Was Kept Out | Levittown, New York and Pennsylvania | 1947-1957
[film 8]
> Now, the second question from the beginning. Who was not allowed in?
The first residents on Long Island signed a lease with a clause, number twenty-five.
It said, in capital letters, that the house could not be used or occupied by anyone other than members of the Caucasian race.
This was not unusual for the time.
The Federal Housing Administration offered mortgages only to developments that were not racially mixed.
So American veterans who were not white could not buy a house in Levittown, however they had served.
William Levitt said he had no room in his heart for racial prejudice.
But, he said, most white people preferred not to live in mixed communities.
He called this attitude perhaps morally wrong, and said he hoped it would someday change.
A group called the Committee to End Discrimination in Levittown campaigned against the policy.
< In 1948, the Supreme Court ruled that deeds requiring racial segregation could not be enforced as law.
[film 8]
Even so, change came slowly.
In Pennsylvania, in 1957, William and Daisy Myers became Levittown's first Black homeowners.
Their arrival was met with racist harassment and mob violence.
The state had to step in.
The Myers family did not give up, and they won national praise for it.
Daisy Myers has been called the Rosa Parks of the North.
