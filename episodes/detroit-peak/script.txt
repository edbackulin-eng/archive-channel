## The Auto Boom | Detroit, Michigan | 1945-1960
> Inside a bustling assembly line, a worker in a hard hat and a blue jumpsuit lifts a massive car part, the rhythmic clank echoing through the plant.
< The smell of oil and metal fills the air, a soundtrack to the city’s prosperity.
> By 1950, American manufacturers produced three’quarters of all automobiles worldwide—8.0 million out of 10.6 million.
> In that same year, 50 percent of U.S. households owned a car, and by 1960, that number rose to 75 percent.
> The auto industry’s success translated into jobs: many families saw their parents working long hours in the factories, earning wages that allowed them to buy a car or a home.
< Workers streamed through turnstiles at the giant Highland Park plant where Henry Ford had revolutionized manufacturing by reducing chassis assembly time by nearly ninety percent with the moving assembly line.
< Outside the factory gates, ambitious entrepreneurs and newly wealthy industrialists built grand Victorian mansions along East Ferry Avenue and Brush Park, showcasing the architectural heights of the Gilded Age.
< Middle‑class families increasingly embraced apartment living in upscale complexes like the Coronado and Davenport apartments, reflecting a modernizing urban lifestyle.

## Music and Culture | Detroit, Michigan | 1955-1965
> In a smoky club, a young singer performs the first Motown hit, the sound of the bass line vibrating through the room.
< The club’s neon sign flashes “Motown” in bright letters, a nickname for the city’s motor industry and its musical heart.
> Berry Gordy, a former record store owner, founded Motown in 1959, turning Detroit into a cultural hub that crossed racial lines.
> By 1960, Motown’s net worth was $61 million, a reflection of the city’s thriving creative scene.
> The music industry’s growth parallelled the auto industry’s, providing another avenue for employment and cultural expression.
< Artists and songwriters gathered inside Hitsville U.S.A. on West Grand Boulevard, where the former photography studio buzzed with tambourines and electric bass lines twenty‑four hours a day.
< Instructor Maxine Powell taught grooming, poise, and social graces to young performers in the artist development department to ensure crossover appeal on white stages.
< Choreographer Cholly Atkins perfected stage routines while vocal coaches like Maurice King shaped the unmistakable harmonies that dominated the Billboard charts throughout the decade.

## The Decline Begins | Detroit, Michigan | 1960-1966
> The skyline now shows empty factory buildings, the once’busy streets quieter, a sign of the economic shift.
< A newspaper headline reads: “Detroit’s Population Declines by 65 Percent.”
< Industrial deconcentration took root as major plants like the Packard Automotive Plant and the massive Hudson Motor Plant closed their doors and were demolished.
< Budget deficits mounted as municipal tax revenues shrank alongside departing residents and migrating corporations.
< Stiff foreign competition and changing national markets strained the traditional manufacturing base that had powered the region for generations.
