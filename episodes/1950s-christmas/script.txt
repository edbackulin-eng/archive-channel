## Chapter 1 | American Homes | 1950-1955

< The 1950s were a time of postwar optimism.
> Families were settling into new homes, many of them owned outright.
< In 1950, 51.3 percent of housing units were owner‑occupied.
> This meant that a large portion of families could afford to put money into holiday décor.
< Across the nation, the population had surpassed 150 million people, representing a gain of 10.4 percent since 1940.
> The median age had increased to 30.2 years, while the under-15 cohort accounted for 26.9 percent of the population.

> In 42.1 percent of households, the head of the family was employed as a craftsman or a machine operator.

> Children under the age of 18 were present in 58.2 percent of all homes, filling the hallways with noise and anticipation as December approached.

## Chapter 2 | Lights and Trees | 1958-1965

< The 1950s saw a shift from candlelit trees to electric lights.
> The first commercially successful artificial tree was not green; it was silver, made of aluminum.
< By 1958, these trees were a common sight in American homes.
> The aluminum tree, with its foil needles and rotating color wheel, became a symbol of the era’s burgeoning consumer culture.
< Manufactured in places like Manitowoc, Wisconsin, by the Aluminum Specialty Company, flagship models like the Evergleam retailed for $25.
> These shimmering, silvery branches were swirled and tapered to a handsome realistic fullness.
< They were fireproof and durable, allowing families to use them year after year without dropping a single needle.
> Because draping electric lights directly through the metal branches could cause a short circuit, homeowners placed a floor‑based color wheel underneath.
< The clear plastic wheel rotated, different colored segments cast a dazzling array of hues throughout the metallic boughs.
> The custom of stringing lights along streets and homes had begun in the early twentieth century, but it was the mid‑century boom that turned it into a holiday staple.
> By the 1960s, tract housing developments across the United States featured roofs and eaves meticulously outlined in weatherproof strings of incandescent bulbs.
< Neighborhood streets glowed warmly at dusk as families walked on concrete sidewalks to admire the outdoor displays on neighboring properties.
> Children pointed eagerly at illuminated wireframe reindeer and glowing plastic Santas mounted securely on manicured front lawns.

## Chapter 4 | Family Life and Work | 1950-1965

< In 1950, women made up 28.8 percent of the workforce, a modest increase from earlier decades.
> The wife in the living room was likely a homemaker, but many women also worked part‑time to support the family.
< By 1960, female participation rose to 37.7 percent.
> Men’s participation had peaked at 86.4 percent in 1950 and fell to 83.3 percent by 1960.
< The average family size hovered around three people.
> In 1950, 58.2 percent of families had children under 18.
< By 1960, that figure was 60.6 percent.
> Unemployment was low, at 4 percent in 1950, and hovered around 5.5 percent in 1960.
< The economy was robust, allowing families to invest in holiday traditions and charitable giving.
> Average family charitable contributions grew from $165 in 1950 to $303 by 1960.
< In bustling urban centers like New York City and Boston, families navigated crowded sidewalks, department store windows, and public transit systems.
> In New York City, 61.0 percent of residents regularly used public transportation to get to work.
> Retail and trade jobs accounted for over 23 percent of employment in both cities, placing workers right at the center of the bustling holiday shopping season.
< Office workers in crisp suits and wool coats hurried through subway turnstiles carrying wrapped parcels tied with red ribbon.
< Department store floorwalkers directed throngs of shoppers past towering display windows adorned with mechanical elves and velvet‑lined shelves.

## Chapter 5 | The Economics of Christmas | 1948-1965

< The Christmas season was a significant retail driver.
> By 1950, department stores were already seeing a surge in sales as early as October.
< The holiday shopping season was a major part of the economy.
> In November 2004, department store sales nationwide rose to $31.9 billion in December 2004, an increase of 54 percent.
< Bookstores and jewelry stores saw even larger surges.
> The rise in consumer spending created jobs.
> Employment in retail rose from 1.6 million to 1.8 million in the two months leading up to Christmas.
< Christmas cards, a staple of the season, were sent in staggering numbers—1.9 billion in the United States each year.
> Live Christmas trees were also in high demand, with millions cut down and transported from tree farms to city lots and neighborhood sidewalks.
< Savings programs like Christmas clubs helped families put away small amounts each week during the year to ensure funds were available when December arrived.
< Bank depositors received special passbooks and token stamps to track their weekly deposits toward the grand total needed for holiday purchases.
< Store clerks stamped receipts and packed fragile glass ornaments into cardboard boxes while cash registers rang continuously in the background.
