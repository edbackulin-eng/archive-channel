## The Death of the Corner Grocery | New York and Memphis | 1946-1950

[film 8]
Before the modern supermarket took over the landscape, buying daily provisions was a slow and deliberate routine.
Historically, retail food sales in the United States had mostly shifted to small corner grocery stores by the 1920s.
[film 3.5]
The standard retail business model required a clerk to fetch products from shelves behind a counter while customers waited in front.
Most foods and merchandise did not come in individually wrapped consumer-sized packages.
[film 3.5]
A clerk had to measure out and wrap the precise amount desired.
Merchants did not post prices, which forced customers to haggle and bargain with clerks to reach fair prices.
[film 3.5]
Early grocery stores were austere and tiny by modern standards, with as few as 450 items.
Shopping for groceries often involved trips to multiple specialty shops, such as a greengrocer, butcher, bakery, fishmonger, and dry goods store.
[film 3.5]
Milk and other items of short shelf life were delivered by a milkman.
During the 1920s, the inefficient American food distribution system meant that the average urban family spent fully one-third of its budget on food.
[pause 0.6]
[film 8]
The concept of a self-service grocery store was developed by Clarence Saunders at his Piggly Wiggly stores, the first true self-service outlet opened in 1916 in Memphis, Tennessee.
Customers entered through a turnstile and walked through four aisles to view the 605 items sold in packages.
[pause 0.6]
[film 8]
By 1950, as the country experienced post-World War II prosperity with unemployment averaging a mere 4 percent, retail food prices had risen sharply from earlier decades.

For a family with an average U.S. family income of $4,237, these prices reflected the postwar economy where food took up 29.7 percent of total spending.

Customers walked the aisles with baskets, selecting items while store workers kept the shelves stocked behind the scenes.
In New York City, households allocated 33.4 percent of their expenditures to food alone, navigating the crowded streets and neighborhood markets before the new supermarket format completely transformed their daily routines.
[film 3.5]
The typical household head in the broader economy was employed as a craftsman or machine operator, bringing home wages that supported growing families in an era of high employment.
Shoppers embraced the new self-service layout, marveling at the sheer variety of goods gathered under a single roof without the traditional wait at a merchant's counter.
[film 3.5]
Small neighborhood retailers struggled against the massive buying power of emerging chains.
Shoppers who had known every clerk by name now drifted silently past towering stacks of goods.
[film 3.5]
The old personal touch of the corner store gave way to efficiency, speed, and lower overhead expenses.

## The Metal Fleet | Oklahoma and Missouri | 1947-1955

[film 8]
The transformation of the grocery store relied on an invention that customers initially rejected.
Sylvan Goldman, owner of the Humpty Dumpty supermarket chain in Oklahoma, invented the first shopping cart in 1937.
Inspired by a wooden folding chair, Goldman put a basket on the seat and wheels on the legs.
[film 3.5]
The invention did not catch on immediately.
Men found them effeminate, and women found them suggestive of a baby carriage.
[film 3.5]
An offended woman informed Goldman that she had pushed her last baby.
After Goldman hired models to push the carts around his store and demonstrate their utility, shopping carts became extremely popular.
[pause 0.6]
[film 8]
In 1946, Orla Watson devised a system for a telescoping nesting shopping cart that did not require assembly or disassembly.
Watson's cart featured a swinging one-way rear door that allowed carts to fit into one another for compact storage.
[film 3.5]
Goldman eventually recognized Watson's invention and paid one dollar in damages for counterfeit, in exchange for which Watson granted Goldman an exclusive operating license.
By the 1950s, these metal fleets crowded the aisles of supermarkets across the country.
[price 17000 1960 | A median owned home]
[film 3.5]
In New York City, where the median owned home was valued at [today 17000 1960] by 1960, space was at a premium, yet shoppers navigated vast store footprints that offered thousands of items under one roof.
The introduction of these carts changed the physical design of retail floors, requiring wider aisles and larger checkout lanes to handle the increased volume of goods.
[film 3.5]
Store owners discovered that giving customers larger wheeled carriers directly increased impulse purchases, as shoppers loaded up on items they had not originally planned to buy.
Mechanics and inventors continued to refine the rolling wire frames, adding child seats in 1947 so parents could manage both toddlers and groceries at the same time.
[film 3.5]
Legal battles over patents and nesting mechanisms occupied the industry for years, but the metal cart remained the enduring symbol of modern self-service shopping.
Maintenance crews hauled stray carts back from distant parking lots using specialized trucks.
[film 3.5]
Manufacturers constantly tinkered with casters, wheel alignments, and zinc coatings to withstand the heavy daily abuse.
Shoppers adapted their own routines around the massive wire baskets, filling them from front to back before joining the growing lines at the newly invented checkout lanes.

## The A&P Empire | New York and New Jersey | 1950-1960

[film 8]
The undisputed giant of this retail revolution was the Great Atlantic & Pacific Tea Company, better known as A&P.
From 1915 through 1975, A&P was the largest grocery retailer in the United States, and until 1965, the largest U.S. retailer of any kind.
[film 3.5]
At its peak in the 1940s, A&P captured 10 percent of total U.S. grocery spending.
The Wall Street Journal noted that A&P was as well known as McDonald's or Google is today, and was the Walmart of its time.
[film 3.5]
In 1930, A&P reached $2.9 billion in sales with 15,000 stores.
[pause 0.6]
[film 8]
As competition from independent supermarkets mounted, A&P began converting its traditional stores into self-serve supermarkets.
By 1950, A&P opened 4,000 larger stores (while phasing out many of its smaller units) after adopting the self-serve supermarket concept in 1936.
For the first time, A&P elected six outside directors onto its board.
[film 3.5]
Yet seeds of decline were already planted.
A&P was starved of capital because most profits were declared as dividends, and management resisted debt financing.
[film 3.5]
While competitors invested in larger, modern supermarkets, A&P was slow to update its retail capital plant.
The company invested heavily in its own manufacturing operations, such as a massive food plant in Horseheads, New York, focusing on private-label goods rather than nationally advertised brands.
[film 3.5]
As the legal and antitrust pressures mounted from the federal government, the Hartford brothers defended their business model by pointing to the lower food costs they provided to American households.
Employees who had spent decades as traditional clerks saw their roles change into specialized positions like stock clerks and cashiers as the old grocery model faded away.
[film 3.5]
Despite its massive scale, the company struggled to adapt to changing consumer preferences in the late 1950s, setting the stage for decades of corporate transformation.
Company executives sat in Manhattan boardrooms while thousands of traditional neighborhood units were quietly shuttered.
[film 3.5]
The massive supply chain hummed with rail shipments and warehouse deliveries moving day and night.
Working families placed their ultimate trust in the uniform red-and-black storefronts that anchored main streets from coast to coast.

## Exporting the Wonder | Rome and Zagreb | 1956-1957

[film 8]
By the 1950s, supermarkets were part of the everyday lives of American consumers, but they remained extremely rare outside the United States.
Most people outside the United States had never seen a supermarket or even heard the term.
[film 3.5]
That changed in 1956 when the U.S. Department of Agriculture presented an American Way exhibit at the International Food Congress in Rome, Italy.
Although it was a modest staging with only about 2,500 items, visitors and international news media were astonished by the mountains of food.
[film 3.5]
In 1957, the U.S. Department of Commerce orchestrated an even grander presentation called Supermarket USA at the Zagreb International Trade Fair in Yugoslavia.
These exhibits were deployed at the height of the Cold War as instruments of American propaganda to demonstrate the superiority of Western Bloc capitalism.
[film 3.5]
Back home, the supermarket continued its relentless expansion.
International visitors walked wide-eyed past towering displays of canned goods and fresh produce, marveling at the abundance made possible by American retail innovations.
[film 3.5]
In the decades that followed, these self-service principles crossed oceans and borders, reshaping food distribution across Europe and beyond.
The legacy of those early garage markets and folding chair carts lived on in every brightly lit aisle around the globe.
[film 3.5]
And the simple act of pushing a metal cart through a grocery store remained a universal daily ritual for millions of people.
Foreign dignitaries adjusted their translation earpieces while stepping past automated turnstiles.
[film 3.5]
Curious onlookers pointed at display cases filled with fresh dairy and cellophane-wrapped meats.
The global exhibition cemented the American retail format as a permanent symbol of postwar abundance and commercial reach.
